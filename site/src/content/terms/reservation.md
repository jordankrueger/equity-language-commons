## Synthesis

The reservation entry is less about whether to use the term and more about **what framing the term carries.** Two distinct framings appear in the corpus, and they are doing different work. The Sierra Club framing is etymological and political: a reservation is land that Tribes *reserved* for themselves through treaty negotiation — not land the federal government allocated to them. Any benefits flowing from that land are treaty rights, not federal benevolence. The DSG framing is descriptive and federal-policy oriented: reservations are areas the US government "reserved as permanent tribal homelands," with US reservation policy dating to 1787, 326 reservations as of 2015, and more than 60 percent of American Indians living off-reservation. Both framings are defensible; they emphasize different load-bearing facts. The Sierra Club framing is the one most often missed in non-Indigenous progressive writing and worth surfacing explicitly when it matters to the story.

NGC and RET both stress a separate point that often goes unstated: **a reservation is not a Native nation.** NGC's definition is the cleanest: "A reservation refers to a Native nation's current land base as defined by the federal government. A Native nation is a sovereign political entity." RET reinforces the sovereignty point — reservations are "nations within," with their own governments, laws, taxation, and justice systems, and tribal sovereignty is inherent rather than delegated. Conflating the two (treating "the reservation" and "the tribe" as synonyms) flattens the political distinction these guides are trying to preserve. NAJA situates reservations within "Indian Country," the umbrella legal term in Title 18 of the US Code, which covers reservations along with other tribal-jurisdiction lands.

Capitalization is straightforward and consistent: lowercase "reservation" as a generic term; capitalize when it appears as part of a specific named reservation, or in a back-reference whose antecedent is specific (NGC's "Cheyenne River Reservation … the Reservation" example). The slang form "rez" is the one term in this entry every source touches lightly except Sierra Club, which is explicit: appropriate within Tribal communities, inappropriate as outsider language. The substitute is the specific place name.

## Audience notes

- **Land-defense and environmental coverage.** Sierra Club's etymological framing — reservations are treaty-reserved land, not government-granted land — is the framing most often missed in non-Indigenous environmental writing about pipelines, mining, or land returns. When the story turns on treaty rights, this distinction is load-bearing; "the government gave them this land" is a substantively different claim than "they kept this land through treaty."
- **Sovereignty framing.** Don't use "reservation" and "tribe" or "nation" interchangeably. The reservation is the land base; the nation is the sovereign political entity. Stories about tribal government, court decisions, or jurisdictional questions should name the nation; stories about land, housing, or local geography may correctly use reservation.
- **The "rez" question.** Don't use "rez" or "on the rez" in writing by or for outside organizations. The term is acceptable in community-internal use and in direct quotation; outside that, the specific place name (the Navajo Nation, the Cheyenne River Reservation) is the cleaner substitute.
