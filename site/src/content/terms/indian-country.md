## Synthesis

**A legal term of art with a popular second life — the corpus's advice is to know which one you're using.** The Indigenous Journalists Association and the Diversity Style Guide give near-identical two-part definitions: Indian Country is first a [Title 18 U.S. Code term](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1151) that defines federal and tribal jurisdiction in crimes affecting American Indians on reservations, and second a popular usage covering reservations and areas with Native populations — IJA adds "lands held within tribal jurisdictions" to that popular sense. The Native Governance Center adds the orthographic rule — capitalize Indian Country — alongside its reminder that a reservation (a federally defined land base) is not the same thing as a Native nation (a sovereign political entity).

The note of caution comes from Sierra Club, and it's about who's speaking. For a non-Native organization, "Indian country" is institutionally sanctioned language — the term the EPA uses for land, in Sierra Club's example — that is sometimes unavoidable to cite but shouldn't be casually adopted as one's own voice; where it must appear, attribute it ("land referred to by the EPA as Indian Country") or find phrasing that circumvents it. (Sierra Club pairs this with a parallel example of citing an action taken by the Bureau of Indian Affairs.) The contrast with the Indigenous-led sources is instructive: IJA, a Native journalism association, presents the term matter-of-factly with its AP-style definition. The term sits differently in Native mouths than in agency prose — the same self-identification gradient that governs "Indian" itself.

In practice the term's two senses pull in opposite directions. In legal and jurisdictional reporting, Indian Country is precise — often the only accurate term for the Title 18 jurisdictional category. In loose popular use ("out in Indian Country") it drifts toward regional color, where the better choice is usually the specific nation, reservation, or place name.

## Audience notes

- **Journalists and editors.** Capitalize it, and use it precisely: for jurisdiction and law it's the correct term; for geography, name the specific nation or reservation instead. Native publications use the term freely — that doesn't automatically license agency-voice copy to do the same.
- **Advocates and agency-facing writers.** When citing agency usage, attribute the term to the institution rather than adopting it ("what the EPA designates Indian Country"), per Sierra Club's institutional-language practice.
