## Synthesis

**"Antisemitism" is the word to use — and the spelling is now closed, without a hyphen.** All three sources define it the same way (prejudice against, hostility toward, or hatred of Jews) and treat it as standard vocabulary. Two of the three foreground a spelling rule that consolidated in the early 2020s: APA, following the Anti-Defamation League, directs writers to spell "antisemitism" without a hyphen, and the Diversity Style Guide documents the same shift, noting the unhyphenated form is favored by the ADL, the World Jewish Congress, and the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum. The reasoning behind the closed spelling is that "anti-Semitism" implies a coherent thing called "Semitism," lending false legitimacy to a 19th-century racial pseudo-category; closing it up refuses that framing.

Racial Equity Tools supplies the structural reading: drawing on Jews for Racial & Economic Justice, it frames antisemitism as an adapting ideology with deep historical roots whose core myth "says that Jews are to blame for society's problems." That is what places antisemitism alongside the commons' other structural concepts — [ageism](/terms/ageism/), [ableism](/terms/ableism/), [classism](/terms/classism/), racism — as a system to be named, not a sensitivity to tiptoe around. It pairs with [islamophobia](/terms/islamophobia/), which Racial Equity Tools describes as "entangled" with antisemitism in the same systems of white supremacy and Christian hegemony.

## Audience notes

- **Journalists and editors.** Spell it "antisemitism" — closed, no hyphen. This is now AP, New York Times, ADL, and USHMM style. Use it as a plain descriptor of anti-Jewish prejudice.
- **Advocates and internal comms.** Name the structure, not just incidents: the sources treat antisemitism as an adapting ideology built on the "Jews are to blame" myth, which is what makes naming it precise rather than vague.
