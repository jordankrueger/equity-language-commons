## Synthesis

**"Dreamer" and "DACA recipient" are both accepted people-terms — the care point is that they are not synonyms.** Define American, the corpus's anchor source on immigration language, defines them in adjacent entries and is explicit about the relationship: DACA recipients are "a subset of 'Dreamers.'" Of the more than two million Dreamers living in the United States — undocumented immigrants who arrived as children or teenagers — only about 580,000 hold DACA protection. Writing that uses the terms interchangeably overstates how many people the program covers and understates how many Dreamers live without any protection at all.

The terms also come from two different things that are easy to conflate. The DREAM Act is legislation — the Development, Relief, and Education for Alien Minors Act, first introduced in 2001, reintroduced in some eleven versions, and never enacted (Define American; Diversity Style Guide). "Dreamer" derives from that acronym and names the people the bill would cover. DACA is an executive program — Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals, announced by President Obama in June 2012 — that grants two-year renewable work permits and deportation relief to a portion of that population, and which can be (and has been) altered by executive action and litigation. The Diversity Style Guide keeps the two in separate, cross-referenced entries for exactly this reason.

On styling, the corpus shows a drift rather than a dispute. SumOfUs (2016) prescribes "DREAMers," retaining the capitalization of the DREAM Act; Define American (2024) treats "Dreamer" as primary with "DREAMer" as a noted variant. SEIU's stylebook handles DACA as a standard acronym headword with its bracketed expansion for first reference. None of the sources treats either people-term as problematic — the guidance is about precision, not avoidance.

## Audience notes

- **Journalists and editors.** Confirm which status a subject actually holds before labeling: "Dreamer" (undocumented, arrived as a child), "DACA recipient" (holds current DACA protection), or neither term if the person prefers otherwise. DACA's legal posture changes with litigation — check the program's current status rather than describing it from memory.
- **Advocates and internal comms.** "Dreamer" carries persuasive framing built into the acronym; it polls well precisely because it was coined to. That's a reason some writers prefer the plainer "undocumented young people" in contexts where the framing should be the audience's conclusion, not the label's.
- **Precision.** DACA is not a pathway to citizenship and confers no permanent status (Define American). Avoid constructions like "DACA citizens" or "legalized through DACA" — they describe something the program does not do.
