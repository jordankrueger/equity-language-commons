## Synthesis

All four guides land in the same place: as a descriptor of people, "colored" is a term to avoid, with one narrow carve-out. NABJ and the Diversity Style Guide share almost the same definition — an archaic term for Black, denoting mixed racial ancestry in some African countries — and the same rule: do not use it "unless referring to official names, historical events or in quotes." The DSG aggregates NABJ's glossary, so the wording overlaps by lineage rather than by coincidence. APA reaches the same conclusion from a different angle, folding "colored" into its broader African American/Black entry and grouping it with "Negro" and "Afro-American" as terms to set aside in favor of "Black." SEIU frames it as a prescribe-and-reject pair: use "persons of color" or "workers of color," never "colored persons."

There is no real dissent across the corpus — the agreement is on both the avoidance and the shape of the exception. The carve-out is consistent and small: proper names (the NAACP keeps "Colored" in its full name), direct quotations, and historical references. Outside of those, the guides point writers to "Black" for the racial group and, where the wider non-white population is meant, to "people of color."

The teaching contrast is exactly that newer term. "Colored people" and "people of color" describe an overlapping group, but they sit on opposite sides of authorship: one is a label that earlier usage attached to people, the other a frame people chose for themselves. SEIU's pairing draws the line directly, prescribing "persons of color" in the same breath that it bans "colored persons." For a communicator, the practical rule is simple — keep "colored" only inside its three exceptions, and use "Black" or "people of color" everywhere else.

## History note

"Colored" has a documented arc in U.S. usage, and the surviving proper names mark its stages. It carries forward in institutional names from an earlier era — the NAACP, founded in 1909, retains "Colored People" in its full title — which is why every guide preserves the official-name exception even as it directs writers away from the term in running prose. The cross-link to [people of color](/terms/people-of-color/) captures the later shift: racial-justice advocates adopted "people of color" as a self-chosen, unifying frame, deliberately distinct from the older noun. Separately, "Coloured" remains a recognized mixed-ancestry identity in parts of southern Africa, a usage NABJ and DSG both note and one governed by local self-identification rather than by this page's U.S.-focused guidance.
