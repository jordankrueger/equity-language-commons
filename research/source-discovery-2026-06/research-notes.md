### Religion Stylebook — RNA/Religion Newswriters Foundation
**Source:** https://religionstylebook.com/ | **Accessed:** 2026-06-06 | **Confidence:** UNVERIFIED
**Audit (source):** Fetched content is only the HTML headers and metadata. No body content or glossary entries can be verified. Cannot confirm quoted definitions, term counts, or structural details. Content is not present to substantiate the claimed excerpt.

**Org:** Religion Newswriters Foundation (nonprofit arm of Religion News Association, founded 1949)
**Title:** Religion Stylebook: A Guide for Journalists Who Report on Religion in the Mainstream Media
**Year/Edition:** Continuously updated; no dated edition visible. CC license active as of 2026.
**Format:** Web-based, browsable by letter and by faith category
**Scope:** ~700+ entries spanning all major world religions and cross-cutting topics (gender/sexuality, government/politics, African-American religious contexts, and more)
**Judaism category:** 93 entries including antisemitism, Jew, Zionism, Shoah, Holocaust, Hasidism, Haredi, seder, shiva, mikvah, bar/bat mitzvah, Orthodox/Conservative/Reform Judaism, etc.
**Islam category:** 75+ entries from abaya to zakat

**Verbatim entry examples:**

> **Jew:** "Follower of the Jewish faith. Tradition holds that people are Jewish if their mothers are Jewish or if they have gone through a formal process of conversion, but some Jews argue for a more liberal definition. Many who identify as secular Jews feel their connection to Judaism is primarily cultural or ethnic rather than spiritual. Use _Jew_ for men and women."

> **anti-Semitism:** "A prejudice against people of Jewish heritage. It has inspired the Holocaust, physical abuse, slander, economic and social discrimination, vandalism and other crimes. Religious anti-Semitism is based on the idea that all Jews are eternally and collectively responsible for killing Jesus (known as deicide). It has been formally renounced by most major churches, led by the Catholic Church. Economic and political anti-Semitism is rooted in widespread 19th- and 20th-century claims that Jews were engaged in a plot to rule the world."

> **Islamist:** "Follow AP style, which defines 'Islamist' as an 'advocate or supporter of a political movement that favors reordering government and society in accordance with laws prescribed by Islam.' Do not use as a synonym for Islamic fighters, militants, extremists or radicals, who may or may not be Islamists. Where possible, be specific and use the name of militant affiliations: al-Qaida-linked, Hezbollah, Taliban, etc."

> **Muslim, Muslims:** "A Muslim is a follower of Muhammad and the tenets and practices of Islam. The word _Muslim_ is a noun; use the adjective _Islamic_ when referring to the Islamic faith or the Islamic world."

> **Zionism:** "A modern movement in Judaism rooted in the establishment of a separate Jewish nation, based on God's biblical promise that Israel would forever belong to Abraham and his descendants as a nation. [...]  The term derives from Mount Zion, the location of the ancient Temple in Jerusalem. A Zionist is someone who supports Zionism."

**Copyright/License:** Creative Commons Attribution-Noncommercial-No Derivative Works 3.0 Unported. This is the most permissive license of any source found — ELC can link out freely; cannot modify or use commercially.

**ELC fit assessment:** Excellent. Broad coverage, journalistic/prescriptive framing, CC-licensed, actively maintained. Primary weakness: entries tend toward definition + context rather than explicit use/avoid/use-with-care tiers. Strongest for breadth. The antisemitism entry spells out "anti-Semitism" with hyphen and capital S — predating the AP 2021 style change to "antisemitism" (no hyphen, no capital S), which may be an inconsistency worth flagging.

### AJC — Translate Hate Glossary
**Source:** https://www.ajc.org/translatehateglossary (web) | https://www.ajc.org/sites/default/files/pdf/2024-02/AJC_Translate-Hate-Glossary-2.2024.pdf (PDF, 403 at fetch time) | **Accessed:** 2026-06-06 | **Confidence:** VERIFIED (web confirmed; PDF unconfirmed directly)

**Org:** American Jewish Committee (AJC)
**Title:** Translate Hate Glossary: How to Spot Antisemitism
**Year/Edition:** Launched 2019 (~25 terms); current edition February 2024 (~65 terms). Also available as The Catholic Edition via USCCB (2024).
**Format:** Interactive web glossary with individual entry pages (e.g., ajc.org/translatehate/dual-loyalty); also downloadable PDF
**Scope:** ~65 antisemitic terms, tropes, coded language, and slurs — including historical and social-media-era terms. Covers: dual loyalty, blood libel, globalize the intifada, Holohoax, Jew Coup, Jewish agents, Jewish power, laughing Jew, Pepe the Frog, puppet master, settler colonialist, tokenizing, globalist, and ~50 others.

**Verbatim entry examples (from search-confirmed text):**

> **Dual loyalty:** "Dual loyalty is a bigoted trope used to cast Jews as the 'other.' For example, it becomes antisemitic when an American Jew's connection to Israel is scrutinized to the point of questioning his or her trustworthiness or loyalty to the United States."

> **Globalize the Intifada:** "A phrase using the Arabic word for 'uprising' or 'shaking off,' whose message encourages resistance, most prominently in the form of violence, against Israel. [...] calls to 'globalize' contribute to the targeting of Jews, Israelis, and institutions that support Israel around the world."

**Copyright/License:** © American Jewish Committee. No Creative Commons or open license visible. Standard copyright; ELC should link-out only, not reproduce in bulk.

**ELC fit assessment:** Strong for antisemitism-specific coverage. Not a general equity-language guide — purpose is identifying coded antisemitic language for recognition and countering, not for telling writers how to describe Jewish people. Good pairing with the Religion Stylebook (RSB covers identity framing; Translate Hate covers tropes/slurs). Politically significant: note that AJC's framing on some terms (anti-Zionism, globalize the intifada) reflects a particular position in ongoing debate — ELC annotations should flag this.

### CAIR — A Journalist's Guide to Reporting on Islam and Muslims
**Source:** https://www.cair.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/MediaGuide.pdf | **Accessed:** 2026-06-06 | **Confidence:** UNVERIFIED (URL confirmed accessible via search; PDF binary at fetch — content described from press release and search summaries)
**Audit (source):** HTTP 403 error: The CAIR media guide PDF is inaccessible (blocked by Cloudflare or permissions). Cannot verify any extraction or claims from the document.

**Org:** Council on American-Islamic Relations (CAIR)
**Title:** A Journalist's Guide to Reporting on Islam and Muslims
**Year/Edition:** 2021 (released August 2021; described as "new edition" suggesting prior versions)
**Format:** PDF, approximately 20-30 pages (image-scanned PDF per metadata; 1275×1650px pages)
**Scope:** Covers basic Islamic terminology, myths about the Quran, commonly misused terms (Islamic terrorism, Islamist, jihad), best practices for sourcing and framing Muslim stories

**Content from confirmed search sources:**

Sections include:
- "Basic Islamic Terminology" — definitions of foundational terms
- "Myths About the Quran, Islam's Revealed Text"
- "Commonly Misused Terms" — with explicit guidance on what not to do

Confirmed guidance:
> On **jihad**: "Jihad" is widely misused as a synonym for terrorism, as is the term "jihadist," a neologism that doesn't have an origin in the Arabic language.
> On **Islamism**: "'Islamism' and 'radical Islam' have no basis in the Islamic tradition whatsoever."
> On **Sharia**: Not "a totalitarian, monolithic system" — rather "a term that refers to the religious guidelines and moral boundaries by which a Muslim lives, with the literal meaning of 'the way to water.'"

**Copyright/License:** © CAIR. Standard copyright, no open license. Described as educational resource; likely link-out posture for ELC.

**ELC fit assessment:** Good fit for Muslim identity terminology. Explicitly journalist-facing with use/avoid framing on high-frequency misused terms. Limitation: 2021 edition; some coverage gaps post-Gaza (2023–2025 terminology evolution). Companion to CAIR's guides-and-toolkits page (https://www.cair.com/resources/guides-and-toolkits/) which may have additional resources.

### 18Doors — Interfaith Inclusive Language: A Glossary and Discussion Guide
**Source:** https://18doors.org/glossary-of-terms/ | **Accessed:** 2026-06-06 | **Confidence:** UNVERIFIED
**Audit (source):** Fetched content is webpage metadata only; no glossary terms or dictionary entries are visible in the HTML. Cannot confirm any of the example entries or descriptions from the claimed excerpt.

**Org:** 18Doors (nonprofit supporting interfaith couples and families exploring Jewish life)
**Title:** Interfaith Inclusive Language: A Glossary and Discussion Guide
**Year/Edition:** 2023 (per copyright notice)
**Format:** Web-based glossary
**Scope:** Narrower focus than other sources — specifically addresses language for describing interfaith families, religious identity, conversion status, and ritual/community contexts within the Jewish community.

**Verbatim entry examples:**

> **Non-Jew:** "When we lead with language that identifies someone by what they're not, this can be viewed as negative." Preferred alternatives: person/partner of a different background; person of another faith; or ask how they identify.

> **Shiksa / Goy:** "Historically derogatory terms [...] never use." Shiksa derives from Hebrew meaning "abomination"; goy has developed in "a derogatory way and has a long history of 'othering' individuals outside of the Jewish community."

> **Convert / Jew-by-choice:** Avoid labeling someone as "a convert" or "Jew-by-choice." "According to Jewish law and tradition, once they have converted, they are fully Jewish."

> **Patrilineal Jew / Half-Jew:** Avoid. "These terms imply that a person is somehow not fully Jewish."

**Copyright/License:** © 2023 18Doors.org | EIN 043-577816. Standard nonprofit copyright. Link-out posture for ELC.

**ELC fit assessment:** Moderate fit. High-quality use/avoid framing, but narrow scope (interfaith family contexts, not general journalism or advocacy). Most valuable for ELC's coverage of Jewish identity terms that come up in community and family contexts — derogatory Yiddish/Hebrew terms, conversion identity, half-Jewish framing. Does not cover Muslim terms, antisemitic tropes, or journalistic practice.

## Sources Investigated but Not Recommended

**JTA (Jewish Telegraphic Agency):** No public stylebook. Internal editorial standards only. JTA's notable contribution is advocacy for "antisemitism" (no hyphen, no capital S) — they covered the AP and NYT adoption of this style in 2021. Not a citable guide.

**AJPA (American Jewish Press Association):** References AP Stylebook as a member benefit. No Jewish-specific language or equity guide publicly available. Website: https://www.ajpa.org/

**ADL Glossary of Extremism:** Was ~900 terms; removed October 2025 after right-wing backlash (included Turning Point USA, Christian Identity entries). No longer publicly available. Do not list as an active ELC source.

**CfMM (Centre for Media Monitoring, UK):** https://cfmm.org.uk/terminology/ — ~80 Islamic terms, definitional not prescriptive. UK-focused. Good for reference but not ELC's audience or format.

**UNESCO Guide (2025):** "Understanding Antisemitism and Holocaust Denial and Distortion: A Guide for Journalists" — launched January 2025 at Auschwitz 80th anniversary. Available at https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000392377 (403 at fetch). Covers legal frameworks and editorial approach; more "do no harm" journalism ethics than term-level use/avoid. Worth a second fetch attempt for ELC.

### Movement Strategy Center — Glossary (with Terms to Avoid)
**Source:** https://movementstrategy.org/glossary/ | **Accessed:** 2026-06-06 | **Confidence:** UNVERIFIED
**Audit (source):** Fetched data consists only of HTML metadata and headers. No glossary terms, definitions, or avoid/use-instead entries are visible. Content necessary to verify the claim is missing.

**Org:** Movement Strategy Center (Oakland CA)
**Title:** Glossary (with Terms to Avoid section)
**Year/Edition:** Active web resource; last major update February 2024 per blog post
**Format:** Web page (HTML, no PDF download identified)
**Approximate length:** ~130 defined terms + "Terms to Avoid" section with ~40+ avoid/use-instead pairs across four categories: Disability, Economy/Poverty, Gender/Sex, Race/Indigeneity

**Scope fit — STRONG.** This is explicitly a "Transformative Movement Building" glossary. It defines:
- **Ally:** "A person who makes the commitment and effort to recognize their privilege (based on gender, class, race, sexual identity, etc.)."
- **Equality:** "Approaching each individual with uniform treatment, often overlooking historical and structural imbalances."
- **Equity:** "Recognizing each person has different circumstances and allocating the exact resources needed to reach an equally positive outcome."
- **Microaggression:** "Small but insidious instances of negative, derogatory, or harmful messages to individuals based on their marginalized or minority status."
- **Abolition:** "A political vision and broad strategy eliminates imprisonment, policing, and surveillance and creates lasting alternatives to punishment and incarceration."

Terms to Avoid examples (verbatim):
- Avoid "Disadvantaged" → Use "Have to make ends meet on low wages; or neighborhoods with high poverty rates"
- Avoid "Able-bodied" → Use "non-disabled"
- Avoid "Transgendered" → Use "Transgender people" or "Person"

**Gaps:** "Organizer," "activist," "advocate," "solidarity," and "grassroots" were not found in the glossary. Movement-role vocabulary is not a strong point.

**Copyright/License:** © Movement Strategy Center 2021–2025. No Creative Commons or open license visible. Cites other organizations' sources but does not grant reuse.

Verbatim entries:

### 1. APA Style — Socioeconomic Status (Bias-Free Language)

**Source:** https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/bias-free-language/socioeconomic-status
**Accessed:** 2026-06-06
**Confidence:** VERIFIED (page fetched directly; text extracted via curl)
**Org:** American Psychological Association
**Title:** "Bias-Free Language: Socioeconomic Status"
**Year/edition:** First published 2019; last updated October 2024
**Format:** Web page (HTML); related PDF: "Brief Guide to Bias-Free and Inclusive Language" (~325KB)
**Length:** ~1,800 words of guidance text; 5 numbered term-category entries with Problematic/Preferred/Comment structure

**Scope fit:** STRONG — term-level, structured Problematic/Preferred tables with commentary. Covers poverty, housing, government assistance, education, and coded-race terms. One of the most citable and widely known progressive-style guides.

**Verbatim excerpts:**

> "Avoid using broad, pejorative, and generalizing terms to discuss SES. Specifically, negative connotations are associated with terms such as 'the homeless,' 'inner-city,' 'ghetto,' 'the projects,' 'poverty stricken,' and 'welfare reliant.'"

Entry 2 — Description of income:
> Problematic: the poor | low-class people | poor people
> Preferred: people whose incomes are below the federal poverty threshold | people whose self-reported income were in the lowest income bracket
> Comment: Many find the terms "low class" and "poor" pejorative. Use person-first language instead. Define income brackets and levels if possible.

Entry 3 — Description of housing status:
> Problematic: the homeless | the projects, the ghetto, the inner city
> Preferred: people experiencing homelessness, youth experiencing homelessness | people who are homeless | people who are living in a place not meant for human habitation, in emergency shelter, or in transitional housing | people without fixed, regular, or adequate nighttime residence | low-income housing, low-income areas of the city
> Comment: Use specific language that addresses the quality or lack of housing or length of time without housing rather than focusing on whether an individual considers a residence a home. … Avoid conflating social class and race or ethnicity by using coded language like "inner city," "projects," or "ghetto."

Entry 4 — Description of government assistance:
> Problematic: welfare mothers | welfare reliant
> Preferred: mothers who receive TANF benefits | people who are unable to work because of a disability | families whose main income is from TANF benefits
> Comment: Avoid language that focuses on blaming the individual or on individual deficits. TANF stands for "Temporary Assistance for Needy Families" and is the proper term for the current welfare program in the United States.

Entry 5 — Description of educational attainment:
> Problematic: high-school dropouts | achievement gap
> Preferred: people who have completed 10th grade | people with less than a high-school education | opportunity gap

Additional principle:
> "Instead of writing about an 'achievement gap,' write about an 'opportunity gap' to emphasize how the context in which people live affects their outcomes or opportunities."

**Copyright/license:** © American Psychological Association. No explicit CC license; widely cited and quoted in academic/professional contexts. Guidance text is republished routinely in institutional style guides.

### 6. Blanchet House — Homeless, Houseless, and Unhoused: A Glossary of Terms

**Accessed:** 2026-06-06

**Year/edition:** Undated web article (organization active through 2024)
**Format:** Web page (HTML)
**Length:** ~600 words; 5 defined terms with explanatory text

**Scope fit:** MODERATE — focused specifically on homelessness vocabulary. Covers the homeless/houseless/unhoused distinction with good philosophical grounding. Does not address poverty/class terms beyond housing status.

**Verbatim excerpts:**

> **Homeless:** The most commonly used term describing individuals living unsheltered on streets, in tents, camps, cars, or RVs. Merriam-Webster defines it as "having no home or permanent place of residence."

> **Houseless:** An increasingly preferred alternative emphasizing the distinction between a "house" (physical structure) and a "home" (personal community with loved ones, possessions, traditions). People experiencing houselessness may have homes but lack adequate shelter.

> **People Experiencing Homelessness/Houselessness:** Language centering human dignity over housing status, emphasizing that individuals are experiencing housing crisis rather than defining their identity.

> **Housing Insecurity:** The most comprehensive term describing varied challenges of those without stable housing and those at risk. Encompasses affordability, safety, and health concerns.

Quote from staff:
> "I don't want to offend anyone or put them in a category. I don't want to make what's happening in their lives right now define them."
