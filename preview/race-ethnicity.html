Race & Ethnicity

15 terms · 1 published · 14 planned

Terminology across racial and ethnic identity. How source guides frame race, where they agree, where community self-identification changes what the "default" term should be, and how the ground has shifted since the mid-2010s.

Overview

What this chapter covers

This chapter gathers the terms most progressive style guides treat as racial or ethnic identifiers, including umbrella terms (BIPOC, people of color), specific-identity terms (Black, Asian, Latinx/Latine, Indigenous), and the capitalization conventions that run through them. Indigenous & Tribal Sovereignty is a separate chapter — most sources that treat Indigenous identity frame it through sovereignty, not race, and the commons follows that framing.

Sources differ more on defaults than on acceptability. Most terms in this chapter have multiple acceptable forms; the real question is which form a writer should use when the individual's preference isn't known. Source-by-source guidance lets readers see the range of positions and choose based on audience.

Cross-cutting principles

Three principles appear in nearly every source guide covering race and ethnicity:

  1. Self-identification is primary. When an individual's preferred term is known, use it — regardless of what a house style would default to.
  2. Prefer specific identifiers over umbrella terms (Latinx over BIPOC; Japanese American over Asian) when the specific identifier is known and relevant.
  3. Capitalization is political. Most sources capitalize Black, Indigenous, Native, Latinx, Asian, etc., and leave white lowercase. The few exceptions are noted per-term.

Latinx

Gender-inclusive term of Latin American identity. Contested on its default usage; stabilizing as "use with care" while Latine ascends for Spanish-speaking audiences.

Positions across 8 sources: use use with care non-preferred evolving

Every source that engages Latinx agrees that self-identification is primary. Where sources diverge is on the default — what to use when the person's preference is unknown. Three positions appear: Latino-default with Latinx on request (Sierra Club, SEIU), Latinx-default without qualification (NGC), and defer-only (TJA, DSG, Racial Equity Tools).

The strongest signal in the data is a time trend: Latine is ascending, Latinx is stabilizing as "use with care." The 2021 guides show no awareness of Latine. The 2023–2024 guides explicitly elevate Latine as the alternative that works better in Spanish. Practitioners writing for Spanish-speaking or bilingual audiences after ~2023 should weight Latine over Latinx.

Hispanic and Latino are not interchangeable, and later guides make that distinction explicit where earlier ones didn't. Hispanic foregrounds Spanish-language heritage (includes Spain, excludes Brazil); Latino foregrounds Latin American geographic origin (excludes Spain, includes Brazil).
