## Source 1: Native Governance Center 2021 — Style Guide

**File:** `source-guides/Native-Governance-Center-Style-Guide-published-2021-02.pdf`
**Canonical URL:** https://nativegov.org/resources/style-guide/
**Confidence:** VERIFIED (archived PDF; URL still live)

### Opening framing — "From Elements of Indigenous Style"

The guide opens by quoting Younging's *Elements of Indigenous Style* as its foundational posture:

> "The goal of Indigenous style is to show respect for Indigenous ways of being in the world in the publishing process and on the page."

> "The process of decolonizing language surrounding Indigenous peoples is not finished; terms, names, and styles continue to evolve."

> "Indigenous style overrules other styles in cases of disagreement."

> "Indigenous style uses capitals where conventional style does not. It is a deliberate decision that redresses mainstream society's history of regarding Indigenous peoples as having no legitimate national identities; governmental, social, spiritual, or religious institutions; or collective rights."

### Capitalization rules (p. 4, Race/Ethnicity/Cultural/Political Groups)

> Capitalize Indigenous, Native, and American Indian. Capitalize other racial and ethnic identifiers, such as Asian and Latinx.
>
> DO capitalize Indigenous or Native when part of the phrase non-Native or non-Indigenous.
>
> DO NOT capitalize the word white.

### Sovereignty and nation-rebuilding (pp. 5–6, Our Work)

> We say nation rebuilding instead of nation building to honor the fact that Native nations have always been sovereign and had their own forms of government prior to colonization.

> Do NOT capitalize sovereignty, self-determination, or self-governance. DO capitalize Treaty Right or Treaty Rights.

### People/nations served (pp. 6–7)

> Refer to the nations we serve as Native nations. Use Tribal nations or Tribes in special circumstances, if necessary.

> Capitalize the words Tribe and Tribal, regardless of whether they appear as part of a proper noun.

> Refer to the people we serve as: Native people, Native, or Indigenous. Use American Indian or Indian when requested by a particular interview subject or when quoting another work.

### Also-capitalized terms (Elements of Indigenous Style, p. 9)

> Creator / First Nations / Métis / Elder / Sundance / Sweat Lodge / Traditional Knowledge

### Inappropriate terminology (p. 10)

> Do not use the former name of the Washington football team. (It's a racial slur.)
>
> Do not refer to Native people as the United States' Native peoples or Native people of the United States. We don't want to imply that Native people are in any way owned by the United States.

### Fair-use quote to pull (<50 words)

Multiple options. Leading candidate, from the opening framing:

> Indigenous style uses capitals where conventional style does not. It is a deliberate decision that redresses mainstream society's history of regarding Indigenous peoples as having no legitimate national identities; governmental, social, spiritual, or religious institutions; or collective rights. (Opening framing, quoting *Elements of Indigenous Style*)

Plus from p. 6:

> Refer to the people we serve as: Native people, Native, or Indigenous. Use American Indian or Indian when requested by a particular interview subject or when quoting another work.

### Synthesis

NGC builds its entire guide on Younging's *Elements of Indigenous Style*, so its posture is: Indigenous style is a distinct editorial system with standing equal to or above conventional style, and capitalization itself is a political act. The guide is Turtle Island–centered (Mni Sota Makoce/Dakota/Ojibwe region) rather than pan-Indigenous. Gives explicit hierarchy: nation > Native/Indigenous > American Indian (only when requested or quoting).

## Source 2: Sierra Club 2021 — Equity Language Guide

**File:** `source-guides/Equity Language Guide Sierra Club 2021.pdf`
**Canonical URL:** https://www.sierraclub.org/equity-language-guide (guide now behind login; historical)
**Confidence:** VERIFIED-ARCHIVED

Sierra Club gives the most extensive coverage of any source in the corpus — a short entry in the Racial/Ethnic Identity section plus a full dedicated chapter on Tribal Sovereignty and Public Lands.

### Racial/Ethnic Identity entry (p. 13)

> Native or Indigenous (Tribal when referring to governments, Tribal or Native when referring to communities within U.S. borders, Indigenous in international contexts)
>
> Whenever possible, use the specific name referring to the relevant Tribe or Band. See the section on "Tribal Sovereignty and Public Lands" for more information. When referring collectively to more than one Tribe, use the term "Tribal Nations."
>
> Be sure to capitalize "Tribe" or "Tribal" as well as "Native" and "Indigenous." Common use and preference on this varies wildly, and we should prioritize the preferences of the people we are referring to. But unless there is reason not to, we should capitalize these terms in order to default to a position that expresses respect.

### Tribal Sovereignty and Public Lands (pp. 16–18)

Chapter opening:

> There are more than 500 distinct Tribal nations within the borders of the United States, each with a unique culture, history, and identity. In order to build true partnerships, we must consider these distinct identities and strive always to respect cultural sensitivities.

Key rules from "Tips on Writing About Tribal Nations":

> Generally speaking, the Sierra Club uses "Tribal" to refer to Native peoples in a U.S. context and "Indigenous" in an international context. "Native" can be used in either context but should always be qualified by "nations," "people" or "peoples."

> Avoid framing that implies that Tribal rights are "given" to Tribes. The federal government does not "give" Tribal nations anything. Through treaties, Tribes ceded their traditional homelands and other properties and rights.

From "Understanding and Respecting Tribal Sovereignty":

> Tribal sovereignty reflects a nation's ability to self-determine, which is a fundamental right. The nation-to-nation relationship between Tribal nations and the federal government is codified into law, including the highest law of the land.

> Treaties are compacts between two nations, typically foreign nations. Article 6, Section 2 of the U.S. Constitution defines treaties as "the supreme law of the land," on a par with the Constitution of the United States itself. Thus, Tribal treaties take precedence over any conflicting state law.

### Cultural Appropriation + Common Phrases to Avoid (p. 18)

> "Indian" or "Indian Country": Similarly, though many Tribal peoples may use "Indian" to refer to themselves, the Sierra Club is not a Tribal organization and should default to using a more formal, respectful term like "Tribal" or "Native." If someone wishes to be identified as "Indian," we should refer to them as "self-identified" (e.g., self-identified Indian activist).

Also flagged to avoid:

> "On the rez" / "spirit animals" / "Eskimo" (except self-ID) / "tribalism" (to describe extreme loyalty) / "low on the totem pole" / "let's powwow" / "circle the wagons"

### Indigenous Peoples' Day (p. 15)

> The Sierra Club does not celebrate Columbus Day because of its association with and celebration of Christopher Columbus and his legacy of colonialism and genocide of Native peoples. Since 2018, the Sierra Club has instead observed Indigenous Peoples' Day.

### Fair-use quote to pull (<50 words)

Leading candidate — the one-line contextual rule:

> Generally speaking, the Sierra Club uses "Tribal" to refer to Native peoples in a U.S. context and "Indigenous" in an international context. "Native" can be used in either context but should always be qualified by "nations," "people" or "peoples." (p. 16)

### Synthesis

Sierra Club operationalizes the Tribal/Native/Indigenous distinction as a **contextual switch** — Tribal for US-governmental, Tribal/Native for US-community, Indigenous for international. This is the sharpest context-based rule in the corpus. The chapter also makes sovereignty and treaty-rights framing central — not just as a terminology note but as an editorial stance: don't say "rights granted to Tribes," recognize ceded homelands, understand treaties as constitutional-level instruments.

## Source 3: NAJA 2017 — Reporting and Indigenous Terminology (poster)

**File:** `source-guides/discovered/naja-indigenous-terminology-2023-06.pdf`
**Canonical URL:** https://naja.com/ (via IJA now — Indigenous Journalists Association)
**Confidence:** VERIFIED (image-based PDF, transcribed via Read tool; cross-verified against GCJT and DSG which both quote NAJA)
**Date note:** The file was saved with `2023-06` in the filename, but PDF metadata shows creation date 2017-04-08. This is NAJA's earlier poster that accompanies their *100 Questions, 500 Answers* reference. Treat as 2017 source. (Schema stress-point: file naming dates don't always match document creation dates.)

### American Indian or Native American

> Either term is generally acceptable and can be used interchangeably, although individuals may have a preference. Native American gained traction in the 1960s for American Indians and Alaska Natives. Over time, Native American has been expanded to include all Native people of the continental United States and some in Alaska. Native American and American Indian can be used interchangeably; however, the term is used only to describe groups of Native Americans - two or more individuals of different tribal affiliation. Journalists should always identify people by their preferred tribal affiliation when reporting on individuals or individual tribes.

### Native

> The term "Native" can be used as an adjective to describe styles; For instance, Native fashion, Native music, or Native art. Journalists should exercise caution when using the word, though, as it is primarily used as slang.

### Indigenous or Aboriginal

> While an official definition of "Indigenous" is not agreed on, the United Nations has developed an understanding of the term based on self-identification, historical continuity to pre-colonial and/or pre-settler societies, links to territories and resources, distinct social, economic and political systems and possession of distinct languages, cultures and beliefs. In the case of the United States, tribal membership or citizenship denotes Indigenous identity. These factors make the words "Indigenous" and "Aboriginal" identities, not adjectives, and NAJA urges outlets to capitalize these terms in order to avoid confusion between indigenous plants and animals and Indigenous human beings. Finally, avoid referring to Indigenous people as possessions of states or countries. Instead of "Wyoming's Indigenous people" try "the Indigenous people of Wyoming."

### Indian Country

> Indian Country is a legal term used in Title 18 of the U.S. Code. It broadly defines federal and tribal jurisdiction in crimes affecting American Indians on reservations. But it also has popular usage, describing reservations, lands held within tribal jurisdictions and areas with American Indian populations.

### Tribal Affiliation

> Reporters should identify Indigenous people by their specific tribes, nations or communities. Headlines and text should also refer to tribes by their proper names, not a catch-all phrase like "Oklahoma Native American Tribe" or "Native American group." While many Indigenous people share a common history of oppression and colonialism, tribal nations are diverse and different; Failing to use the actual name of the tribe you are reporting on is neither accurate, fair or thorough and undermines diversity by erasing the tribe's identity.

Closing rule:

> Reporters unsure of names and terms should ask the Indigenous people they are reporting on which words are preferred, or access tribal government websites for correct nomenclature.

### Fair-use quote to pull (<50 words)

From the Indigenous or Aboriginal section:

> The words "Indigenous" and "Aboriginal" are identities, not adjectives, and NAJA urges outlets to capitalize these terms in order to avoid confusion between indigenous plants and animals and Indigenous human beings. Avoid referring to Indigenous people as possessions of states or countries. (NAJA Reporting and Indigenous Terminology, 2017)

### Synthesis

NAJA is the canonical short reference. Two headline moves: (1) **capitalization is a semantic distinction**, not ornamental — lowercase "indigenous" for plants/animals, uppercase "Indigenous" for people. DSG, GCJT, NGC, and Sierra Club all directly or indirectly inherit this rule from NAJA. (2) **"Native" as adjective only, primarily slang** — stricter than the broader recommendation to use "Native" as a noun. Other guides (NGC especially) diverge from this on "Native people" as a preferred self-reference. The divergence is less about disagreement than about audience: NAJA is addressing newsroom copy, NGC is addressing Native-org self-description.

## Source 4: SumOfUs 2016 — A Progressive's Style Guide

**File:** `source-guides/discovered/sumofus-progressive-styleguide-2016.pdf`
**Canonical URL:** None (SumOfUs merged into Ekō 2022; orphaned).
**Confidence:** VERIFIED-ARCHIVED

SumOfUs's Indigeneity/Ancestry section (pp. 23–26) is the broadest international framing in the corpus — covers US, Canada, and Australia separately.

### Opening framework

> Decolonization demands an Indigenous framework and a centering of Indigenous land, Indigenous sovereignty, and Indigenous ways of thinking.
>
> — Eve Tuck and K. Wayne Yang, *Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor*

### Specific Recommendations (p. 24)

> "Indigenous" is internationally inclusive for all Indigenous peoples.
>
> Whenever possible, use a specific name (e.g., Cherokee and Inuit). If you are not aware of the preferred name, whenever possible, ask.
>
> The term "Indian" is outdated and should be replaced by the term "First Nation" except in the following cases: in direct quotations; when citing titles of books, works of art, etc.; in discussions of history where necessary for clarity and accuracy; in discussions of some legal/constitutional matters requiring precision in terminology; in discussions of rights and benefits provided on the basis of "Indian" status; and in statistical information collected using these categories (e.g., the Census).
>
> The term "Eskimo" is outdated and has been replaced by "Inuit."

### Pan-global framing (pp. 24–25)

On hierarchy framing:

> Avoid vocabulary and usage that carries hierarchical valuation, describes Indigenous peoples as "belonging" to Canada, the United States, or Australia, etc., and other usages that may denote inferiority.

On blood quantum:

> Terms that distinguish "racial purity" come from a colonized and government-invented caste system. For example, the sort of blood quantum system apparent in South America and imposed by the Spanish Conquistadors, with terms like "Mestizo" from the Casta system, was used explicitly to separate people into classes.

On Canada (p. 25):

> "Aboriginal Peoples" refers to all the Aboriginal people collectively, without regard to their separate origins and identities (including Métis, First Nations, and Inuit). "Native Peoples" is a collective term to describe the descendants of the original peoples of North America. The term is increasingly outdated like Aboriginal (particularly when used as a noun) and is losing currency. The term "First Nation(s)" is widely used and has for the most part replaced the term "Indian."

### Y/? table (p. 26)

**Used:** Aboriginal Peoples (in Australia) / First Nations (in Canada) / First Peoples / Indigenous (for global references) / Inuit (not Eskimo) / Inuk (singular of Inuit) / Native Americans (for the Americas) / Original Peoples

**Avoided/questioned:** Criollo / Eskimo (use Inuit) / folklore (if used to describe a belief system as less valid) / full-blood / half-breed / half-caste / Indian (unless quote or already-established name) / Indio / magic (if describing a belief system as less valid) / Mestizo / Multo / myth (if describing a belief system as less valid) / Negro / Pardo / part-aboriginal / part-Indian / part-native / sorcery / Squaw (unless quote or established name) / superstition(s) (if describing a belief system as less valid) / Zambo

### Fair-use quote to pull (<50 words)

> "Indigenous" is internationally inclusive for all Indigenous peoples. Whenever possible, use a specific name (e.g., Cherokee and Inuit). If you are not aware of the preferred name, whenever possible, ask. (p. 24)

And:

> Avoid vocabulary and usage that carries hierarchical valuation, describes Indigenous peoples as "belonging" to Canada, the United States, or Australia, etc., and other usages that may denote inferiority. (p. 24)

### Synthesis

SumOfUs's stance is **global first, national second** — Indigenous as the pan-country umbrella, with country-specific conventions layered on top (First Nations in Canada, Aboriginal in Australia, Native American in the US). The decolonization framing is explicit: this isn't terminology hygiene, it's participation in a political project. Also unusual: the guide addresses Global South colonial-caste terminology (Mestizo, Criollo, Pardo, Zambo) under the same Indigeneity heading — a broader scope than the U.S.-journalism-focused guides (NAJA, DSG, NGC).

Native American gained traction in the 1960s for American Indians and Alaska Natives. ... Journalists should always identify people by their preferred tribal affiliation when reporting on individuals or individual tribes. ... ### "First Nations" (line 1361)

This phrase came into common usage in the 1970s and '80s and generally replaced the term Indian to refer to Indigenous people of Canada. The term is not widely used in the U.S. Updated February 2021

## Source 6: Racial Equity Tools 2023

**File:** `source-guides/discovered/racial-equity-tools-glossary-2023.md`
**Canonical URL:** https://www.racialequitytools.org/glossary
**Confidence:** VERIFIED

### "Indigenous populations" (line 2085, quoting UN Cobo report)

> Indigenous populations are composed of the existing descendants of the peoples who inhabited the present territory of a country wholly or partially at the time when persons of a different culture or ethnic origin arrived there from other parts of the world, overcame them and, by conquest, settlement, or other means, reduced them to a non-dominant or colonial condition; who today live more in conformity with their particular social, economic, and cultural customs and traditions than with the institutions of the country of which they now form part.
>
> **SOURCE:** UN Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues (2010), originally presented in the preliminary report of the Special Rapporteur of the UN Commission on Human Rights, José Martínez Cobo (1972).

### "Indigenous Sovereignty" (line 2128)

> It is distinguishable from Tribal Sovereignty in that it is not a nation-state recognition of inherent sovereignty under nation-state dominion. Rather, it arises from Indigenous Traditional Knowledge, belonging to each Indigenous nation, tribe, first nation, community, etc. It consists of spiritual ways, culture, language, social and legal systems, political structures, and inherent relationships with lands, waters, and all upon them. Indigenous sovereignty exists regardless of what the nation-state does or does not do. It continues as long as the People that are a part of it continue.
>
> **SOURCE:** Indigenous Environmental Network, "What is: Indigenous Sovereignty and Tribal Sovereignty," June 2020.

### "Tribal Sovereignty" (line 4580)

> Refers to the legal recognition in the United States of America law of the inherent sovereignty of American Indian Nations. The law applied to American Indians by the US government is called Federal Indian Law. ... Indian reservations are recognized as what can be termed "nations within." Each has its own government and sovereign powers to make laws, tax, etc. and most also have their own tribal justice system, also based upon their inherent powers.
>
> It is important to understand that tribal sovereignty is not delegated from the US government. Treaties do not create tribal sovereignty. Treaties are an affirmation between Indian nations and nation-states.

### Fair-use quote to pull (<50 words)

From Indigenous Sovereignty:

> [Indigenous Sovereignty] is distinguishable from Tribal Sovereignty in that it is not a nation-state recognition of inherent sovereignty under nation-state dominion. Rather, it arises from Indigenous Traditional Knowledge, belonging to each Indigenous nation, tribe, first nation, community, etc. Indigenous sovereignty exists regardless of what the nation-state does or does not do. (RET, quoting Indigenous Environmental Network, 2020)

### Synthesis

RET's distinctive contribution is the explicit split between **Tribal Sovereignty** (a settler-state legal recognition) and **Indigenous Sovereignty** (an inherent, pre-colonial political condition that exists regardless of state recognition). No other guide in the corpus names this distinction so cleanly. Sierra Club's Tribal Sovereignty chapter operates within a purely US-legal frame; NGC centers sovereignty as editorial stance but doesn't define the two levels separately; NAJA treats sovereignty as background. RET's entry is the corpus's single best treatment of the conceptual substrate.

## Schema stress-points encountered (feeding schema v0.3 decision)

This term hit harder than either prior test term. Running list:

1. **Term-cluster size** — Indigenous has a genuine cluster: Indigenous / Native / Native American / American Indian / First Nations / Aboriginal / Alaska Native / Inuit / Indian / Tribal. Some are aliases (Native/Indigenous in certain contexts), some are related terms with distinct scopes (First Nations = Canada only; Alaska Native = U.S. umbrella for several groups). Unlike Latinx (where variants map roughly cleanly onto separate files) or unhoused/homeless (where the cluster was small and tight), Indigenous sits in the middle: **some relationships warrant separate files (Indigenous ↔ First Nations, two different geographic-institutional terms), some don't (Native / Indigenous as near-synonyms under certain house styles).** Schema v0.2's `related_terms` with a `relation` enum handles this, but the enum needs one more value: `geographic-variant` (First Nations for Canada, Aboriginal for Australia, Native American for US — same political function, different national context).

2. **Chapter-level guidance** — Sierra Club's Tribal Sovereignty chapter isn't a term entry; it's an editorial chapter on a concept (sovereignty) that appears as a section heading rather than a specific term ruling. The entry schema is term-centric; sovereignty/treaty-rights framing doesn't naturally fit as a `guidance[]` row for "Indigenous." This reinforces the earlier (term #2) proposal for some kind of `chapter_level_framing[]` or a separate chapter-content type.

3. **Meta-source attribution** — DSG and GCJT both directly quote NAJA inside their own entries. When citing DSG, do we also cite the underlying NAJA source? The schema has `source_url` per guidance entry, but not a `derived_from` or `quotes_source` field. For now, the right move is: cite DSG as the direct source, and mention NAJA attribution in `paraphrase`. **v0.3 candidate:** optional `derived_from: [org_slug]` per guidance entry.

4. **Date-metadata mismatch on archived files** — NAJA file is saved with `2023-06` in name but PDF metadata shows 2017. The schema already has `year` per guidance; this is a filesystem-hygiene issue, not a schema issue. Noted for MANIFEST.md correction.

5. **External references to other guides** — SumOfUs, TJA, GCJT all point to the Reporting in Indigenous Communities guide / NAJA reporting guides / Tribal Nations Media Guide. The `external_references[]` prototype from term #2 fits cleanly — validated again.

6. **Paid/book references cited by guides** — NGC's guide is structurally built on Younging's *Elements of Indigenous Style* (2018, paid book). NGC can't host it; we can't host it; but the book materially shapes NGC's positions. MANIFEST already handles this (cited-as-reference-only category). No schema change; but the term entry should mention Younging when synthesizing NGC's posture.

7. **Confidence level for WebFetch summaries** — APA is a WebFetch summary, not verbatim archive. That's not really `PARTIAL` in the schema's sense (source found but exact wording differs) — it's "source found, our archive is a paraphrase-only summary." Possibly warrants a new enum value: `SUMMARY-ONLY` (meaning: we have faithful paraphrase but no verbatim archive). **v0.3 candidate.**

8. **Categories for this term:** "indigenous-identity-and-sovereignty" as a top-level category makes more sense than "race-ethnicity" alone — the sovereignty framing is load-bearing. This term pushes against the Latinx-derived category set. Free-form categories work for now; formal taxonomy discussion will need to grapple with whether sovereignty is a distinct chapter.

### Decision posture going into schema v0.3

After three test terms, the v0.3 candidate list is stable. Ready to lock. Concrete proposals:
- Add top-level `external_references[]` (validated on terms #2 and #3)
- Add top-level `methodological_context[]` (validated on terms #2 and #3)
- Add optional per-guidance `derived_from: [org_slug]` for citation-chain clarity (new in term #3)
- Extend `related_terms.relation` enum with `geographic-variant` (new in term #3)
- Extend `confidence` enum with `SUMMARY-ONLY` for WebFetch-summary archives (new in term #3)
- Keep `chapter_level_framing[]` as a **deferred** question — it's not a term-schema issue, it's a chapter-schema issue, and we don't have a chapter schema yet.
