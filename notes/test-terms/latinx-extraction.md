- Quote — Latine (<50 words, exact): "Latine is a gender-neutral or non-binary alternative to Latino and Latina. It flags the 2019 Pew finding that only 3% of U.S. Hispanic/Latino adults used "Latinx" to describe themselves, and documents Latine as the emerging alternative preferred in Spanish-speaking communities due to its pronounceability. Hispanic and Latino are treated as related but non-interchangeable (Spanish-language descent vs. Latin American origin).
- Confidence: VERIFIED
- Notes: The 3%/23% Pew data point (Dec. 2019) is the most-cited adoption rate figure in this space — it appears in DSG and is relevant for any synthesis. Latine entry explicitly notes Spanish-speaking origin of the term, which distinguishes it from Latinx (English-language internet coinage). Both Latine and Latina/Latino entries were updated March 2024, indicating active maintenance.

## Cross-source synthesis

### Where guides agree

1. **Self-identification is primary.** Every guide that addresses the Latinx/Latino/Hispanic cluster (Sierra Club, TJA, Diversity Style Guide) agrees: use the term a person uses for themselves. This is the floor-level consensus.

2. **Hispanic and Latino are not interchangeable.** Sierra Club and Diversity Style Guide both draw the distinction explicitly — Hispanic = Spanish-language descent; Latino/a/x = Latin American geographic origin. This means Brazil is excluded from "Hispanic" but included in "Latino." Only Casey lists them as flat equivalents (2013, pre-debate).

3. **Both terms are capitalized.** SEIU, Casey, Native Governance Center all capitalize these terms. This is not contested.

4. **Latinx is contested in the broader community, not in progressive org style.** Progressive style guides generally accept Latinx as a valid term; the debate is about *when* to default to it vs. Latino/Latina. The Pew 3%/23% adoption data (DSG) signals that Latinx is primarily used by progressive institutions, not by the community itself — relevant for audience-aware guidance.

### Where guides diverge

1. **Default term when no individual preference is known:**
   - Sierra Club defaults to "Latino/Latina"
   - SEIU defaults to "Latino/Latina" (no Latinx in the stylebook)
   - Casey (2013) defaults to Hispanic or Latino equally
   - TJA does not establish a default — identity deferral only
   - NGC uses Latinx as its example term (implicit default)

2. **How prominently Latine is surfaced:**
   - TJA: explicitly names Latine as preferred by some, mentioned first before Latinx in the section header
   - Diversity Style Guide: full separate entry for Latine (updated March 2024), describes it as increasingly preferred by Spanish speakers
   - Sierra Club (2021): does not mention Latine (pre-dates the term's rise)
   - SEIU: no mention
   - Casey: no mention
   - NGC: no mention

3. **Whether Latinx gets a "use with care" framing vs. unconditional acceptance:**
   - Sierra Club: conditional (self-ID, partner org use, or explicit project conversation)
   - TJA: conditional (self-description only)
   - NGC: unconditional (uses as example without qualification)
   - SEIU/Casey: Latinx absent entirely

### What's evolving

The clearest trajectory in this corpus: **Latine is ascending, Latinx is stabilizing as "use with care."**

- The 2021 guides (Sierra Club, NGC) show no awareness of Latine
- The 2023–2024 guides (DSG, TJA) explicitly elevate Latine as an alternative that works better in Spanish-speaking communities
- Latine's advantage over Latinx: it can be pronounced in Spanish, it works grammatically in Spanish (following the gender-neutral "e" pattern), and it originated in Spanish-speaking LGBTQIA+ communities rather than as an English-language internet construction

The Racial Equity Tools framing ("depends on geographic location, age, generation, political orientation") captures where the discourse actually is: this cluster has no single right answer across all contexts.

## Flagged for schema review

The following issues emerged from fitting this data into the proposed schema. These are the most valuable findings for schema iteration.

### 1. The `quote` field cannot capture multi-part guidance

Several guides give compound guidance: a default, a caveat, and an exception. Sierra Club's Latinx entry is the clearest example — it has a two-paragraph structure that cannot be reduced to one quote under 50 words without losing material meaning. The schema's single `quote` field forces a choice between the default statement ("Sierra Club defaults to 'Latino'") and the Latinx-specific guidance — both are load-bearing.

**Suggested schema addition:** `quote_additional` (optional array of additional quotes from the same source on the same term). Or: allow `guidance[]` entries to have multiple objects per source.

### 2. The `recommendation` enum doesn't handle "preferred over another term"

Sierra Club's position on Hispanic is not `avoid` — it's "don't default to this; prefer Latino; use it if the person self-identifies." That's weaker than `use-with-care` (which implies the term itself needs care) and stronger than `use` (which implies full endorsement). It's really "non-preferred-but-acceptable."

Similarly, Latino/Latina vs. Latinx is not a use/avoid binary — it's a hierarchy of defaults.

**Suggested enum addition:** `non-preferred` — source accepts the term but recommends a different form as the default. This would distinguish "avoid Hispanic" from "prefer Latino over Hispanic."

### 3. The schema has no field for adoption/usage data

The Pew 3%/23% finding (Dec. 2019 — only 3% of Hispanic/Latino adults used Latinx for themselves) is cited by DSG and is crucial context for any practitioner guidance. It's not a quote from the style guide, it's external data *cited by* the style guide. The schema has no home for this.

**Suggested schema addition:** `context_data` (optional) — a field for empirical data points that style guides cite as the basis for their guidance.

### 4. The `quote_loc` field needs to handle anchor IDs and updated-at timestamps, not just page numbers

TJA's entry includes `updated="2023-08-25"` as a DOM attribute on the section header — that date is the authoritative version date for this specific entry, different from the guide's overall publication date. The schema captures `year` at the guidance level, but doesn't have a `entry_updated` or `quote_loc_anchor` field.

**Suggested schema addition:** `entry_updated` (date) — the last-updated date for a specific term entry, separate from the guide's publication year. Especially relevant for web-native guides that version entries individually (TJA, DSG, Diversity Style Guide).

### 5. The `aliases` field at the term level mixes things that arguably need separate entries

"Latinx," "Latine," "Latino/a," "Hispanic," and "Chicano/a" are aliases in the proposed schema — but they receive meaningfully different treatment from multiple guides. DSG has *separate full entries* for each. TJA treats Latine as the emerging preferred form. This means an alias relationship in the schema might actually need to be a "see also" or "related term" relationship, with Latine potentially warranting its own slug.

**Suggested schema change:** Distinguish `aliases` (truly interchangeable: Latina, Latino, Latino/a, Latinx are forms of the same thing) from `related_terms` (Latine is a distinct term that some communities prefer; Chicano/a is a separate identity with distinct history). The current schema conflates these.

### 6. No field for "audience caveat"

Sierra Club, DSG, and Racial Equity Tools all flag that the right term depends on the audience — geographic region (Hispanic is more common east of the Mississippi; Latino west), generation (younger cohorts more likely to use Latinx/Latine), and political orientation. The schema has `tags` but no structured way to encode audience-specific guidance.

**Suggested schema addition:** `audience_notes` (already flagged as "still open" in schema.md) — this test term confirms it's needed, not optional.

### 7. The `confidence` enum works but needs a "sourced-but-paywalled" value

The Sierra Club guide is now behind a login per MANIFEST. The quotes here were extracted from Jordan's archived copy, which is VERIFIED against the PDF. But if someone tries to verify the canonical URL, they'll hit a paywall. This is different from UNVERIFIED (where no source was found) and different from PARTIAL (where the wording differs). The source exists and is confirmed, but the canonical URL is not publicly confirmable.

**Suggested enum addition:** `VERIFIED-ARCHIVED` — quote confirmed from archived copy; canonical URL no longer publicly accessible.
